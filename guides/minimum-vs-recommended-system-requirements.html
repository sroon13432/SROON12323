Minimum vs Recommended System Requirements: What Actually Matters

Updated 22 November 2026 9 min read 1,186 words · data from 28,163 verified game requirement sets

Minimum is a target, not a measurement. Here is what each column is written for, which numbers to trust, and the naming traps that make people buy the wrong card.

Every requirement table is written against an unstated frame rate, an unstated resolution and an unstated preset. Publishers rarely say which, and the industry convention is not consistent: one studio's minimum is 1080p at 30 FPS on low, another's is 720p at 30 FPS with upscaling, and a third is 1080p at 60 FPS on lower settings. The difference between those three readings is enormous, and it is entirely invisible on the store page.

This guide explains what each column is usually written against, which parts of the table decide the outcome, and the naming traps that make two cards with the same model number perform completely differently.

What each column is actually written for

ColumnTypical targetWhat you should expect
Minimum1080p, low preset, 30 FPS, sometimes 720p or with upscalingThe game runs and is playable. It will look much simpler than the marketing footage, and it will not hold 60 FPS
Recommended1080p, high preset, 60 FPSThe intended experience. This is the column that answers can I enjoy this game
Ultra or 4K (when listed)1440p or 4K, maximum settingsTwo to three tiers above the recommended card, and often written for 30 to 60 FPS at that resolution

A useful habit: read the recommended column as the real requirement and the minimum column as the notice that the game exists. If you clear the recommended GPU and RAM, buy with confidence. If you only clear the minimum, you are buying a 30 FPS experience with low settings, and you should decide whether that is acceptable before you spend anything.

The GPU name is the least reliable line

Graphics card names are reused across memory configurations, laptop and desktop versions, and even different chips. Four examples that catch people every year:

  • GTX 1060 3 GB versus GTX 1060 6 GB. The 3 GB version is not just smaller: it has fewer shader units and is around ten percent slower before the memory limit even matters.
  • RTX 3060 8 GB versus 12 GB. The 8 GB version has a narrower memory bus and is materially slower, despite the identical model number.
  • RTX 4060 desktop versus RTX 4060 Laptop GPU. The laptop version has fewer cores and a lower power budget, and it is not the card the requirement table was written against.
  • Intel integrated graphics names. Iris Xe 80EU and 96EU share a name and differ by twenty percent, and the same chip performs differently at 15 W and 28 W.

The fix is to compare the requirement against a performance tier rather than a name, and to check the memory figure on your own card rather than the marketing label. Our per-game pages show the requirement as published, and our GPU guides group cards into tiers so a card can be compared against a name three generations old.

The CPU line matters more than people think

The published CPU requirement is usually generous in headline terms and misleading in practice. A game that lists an eight-year-old quad-core as its minimum can still be heavily CPU-limited in busy scenes, because the requirement refers to a scene the tester chose. Open-world games, simulation and anything with many AI agents are usually CPU-bound before they are GPU-bound, and dropping the resolution will not raise the frame rate at all.

If your CPU matches the minimum and your GPU matches the recommended figure, you are likely to be CPU-limited in the busiest areas even though the table looks fine. That combination is the most common cause of a machine that benchmarks well and stutters in the places that matter.

RAM, VRAM and storage: read them together

  • System RAM in the table means total installed, not free. A 16 GB requirement means 16 GB in the machine, with Windows sharing it.
  • VRAM is listed separately when the game expects it, and it is a hard ceiling. A 6 GB requirement on an 8 GB card is comfortable, and a 10 GB requirement on an 8 GB card means textures must drop a step.
  • Storage is the most skipped line. If it says SSD required, a mechanical drive will produce texture pop-in and long loads even when everything else is fine.
  • DirectX version tells you about feature support rather than performance, but DirectX 12 Ultimate implies hardware ray tracing and mesh shading.

How to read a table in 30 seconds

  1. Find the recommended GPU and place it on a performance ladder. Where does your card sit relative to it?
  2. Find the recommended RAM. Is your installed total at or above it?
  3. Check the VRAM figure if one is listed. Does your card carry that much?
  4. Check whether storage says SSD. Is your install drive one?
  5. Look at the CPU line. Is your CPU at least as fast per core as the one named?
  6. If all five are yes, the game will run at its intended settings. If two or more are no, expect to lower the resolution or the preset.
The most useful question

Not can I run it, but at what settings will it run the way the recommended column describes. That is the number the publisher tested and the one your purchase decision should be based on.

Frequently asked questions

Does meeting the minimum requirements mean the game runs at 60 FPS?

Almost never. The minimum column is written for a playable experience, which in most cases means 1080p at low settings and around 30 FPS, sometimes at a lower resolution or with upscaling. If 60 FPS is the goal, the recommended column is the one to meet.

Why do two graphics cards with the same name perform differently?

Because manufacturers reuse model names across memory sizes, chip revisions and laptop versions. A GTX 1060 3 GB has fewer cores than the 6 GB version, an RTX 3060 8 GB has a narrower bus than the 12 GB version, and a laptop RTX 4060 is not the desktop card. Always check the memory figure and whether the card is a laptop part.

Are recommended requirements usually accurate?

They are accurate for the configuration the publisher tested, which is normally 1080p at high settings and 60 FPS. They are not updated for later patches or for hardware released after launch, and they say nothing about 1440p or 4K. Treat them as a reliable floor for the intended experience rather than a precise measurement.

What should I upgrade first if I fail both columns?

The GPU, unless the CPU is more than a few generations behind the requirement. A modern budget card in an old platform will be held back by the CPU in exactly the games that need the upgrade. If both are far behind, a whole platform replacement is usually better value than upgrading one part.
