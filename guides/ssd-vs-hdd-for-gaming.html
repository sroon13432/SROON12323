SSD vs HDD for PC Gaming: Load Times, DirectStorage and Space

Updated 27 November 2026 9 min read 1,163 words · data from 34,704 verified game requirement sets

Load times, texture streaming and the games that now require an SSD: what the difference actually costs, and how much space you really need.

For most of PC gaming history, storage only affected loading screens, and a hard drive was a mild inconvenience. That stopped being true somewhere around 2023. Modern games stream textures while you move, and when storage cannot keep up the result is not a longer load, it is visible pop-in and stutter in the middle of play. A growing number of releases now list an SSD as required rather than recommended.

Here is what the difference actually looks like, which games have crossed the line, and how to plan capacity so the answer is not one game per drive.

The load time numbers are the ones people quote, but the streaming row is the one that matters. A hard drive delivers roughly 100 to 160 MB per second sequentially, and far less when the game requests many small files at once, which is exactly what asset streaming does. An SSD delivers thousands of operations per second regardless of file size, which is why it fixes hitching that a faster GPU cannot.

SATA versus NVMe: the honest difference

In most games, the difference between a SATA SSD and an NVMe SSD is a few seconds on a load screen and nothing during play. Synthetic benchmarks show a five to ten times gap in throughput, but games rarely request data at those rates for long. The visible difference appears in three situations: games built around DirectStorage, very large single-file asset loads, and any workflow that copies large files.

That means an upgrade from a hard drive to a SATA SSD captures almost all of the real-world benefit. Do not skip a good SATA drive waiting to afford an NVMe one unless the game you are playing specifically requires NVMe.

DirectStorage and the games that require an SSD

DirectStorage lets a game move asset data from an NVMe drive to GPU memory with far less CPU involvement, and it depends on an NVMe drive, a DirectX 12 GPU and a recent Windows build. Where a game uses it, load times drop sharply and streaming becomes effectively invisible. Where a game requires an SSD in its requirement table, it is usually because the world is built around streaming rather than pre-loaded levels.

  • Games with SSD required in the requirement table will not run acceptably on a mechanical drive, regardless of GPU.
  • A SATA SSD satisfies almost all of these requirements; only a handful of DirectStorage titles specifically want NVMe.
  • Check the requirement text on the game page rather than assuming. Some say SSD recommended, which is a different situation.
  • If your drive is over 90 percent full, SSD performance degrades. Leave 10 to 15 percent free on the game drive.
The cheap fix

If a game stutters while you move and your GPU utilisation is not pegged, check what drive it is installed on before buying any hardware. Moving it takes minutes and costs nothing.

How much space you actually need

Install sizes have roughly doubled since 2020. A large open-world release in 2026 commonly lands between 100 and 160 GB, and a few exceed 200 GB. Add a 10 to 20 percent patch allowance and the practical figure for a single big game is 120 to 180 GB.

Steam's library manager lets you move an installed game between drives without redownloading, which is the cheapest way to live with a small SSD: keep two or three games on it, park the rest on a mechanical drive, and move them across the night before you plan to play. Two drives is a better use of money than one enormous one.

Choosing a drive without overpaying

  1. Match the form factor to the slot: M.2 2280 for desktops and most laptops, M.2 2230 for small handhelds and some thin laptops. A 2280 drive will not physically fit a 2230 slot.
  2. Prefer a drive with DRAM cache or a modern controller with HMB support. DRAM-less QLC drives slow down badly once their cache is exhausted, which is exactly what a 100 GB install does.
  3. For a games drive, PCIe 4.0 is plenty. The premium for PCIe 5.0 buys nothing in games and costs heat and money.
  4. Check the endurance rating if the drive will also hold video or project files. Games are read-heavy, so endurance matters less than for a scratch disk.
  5. Keep a mechanical drive only for archival and media. Do not install games to it in 2026 except for very old titles.

Frequently asked questions

Is an SSD worth it for gaming?

Yes, and it is the upgrade with the most obvious effect per pound. Load times drop by two thirds or more, and games that stream assets while you move stop hitching. The difference between SATA and NVMe is small; the difference between either and a mechanical drive is large.

Do I need an NVMe drive for DirectStorage?

DirectStorage requires an NVMe drive to use its fastest path, along with a DirectX 12 GPU and a current Windows version. Games that merely list an SSD as required will run on a SATA SSD. Only a handful of titles specifically require NVMe.

How much free space should I leave on my game drive?

At least 10 to 15 percent, and more on a DRAM-less drive. SSDs slow their write path as they fill, and games that patch frequently need room to write. A 1 TB drive with 850 GB used is fine; one with 970 GB used is not.

Can I move a Steam game to another drive without downloading it again?

Yes. In Steam, right-click the game, open Properties > Installed Files, and choose Move install folder. Steam copies the files to the new library and updates its records. It takes minutes rather than hours and is the fastest way to test whether storage is causing a stutter.
