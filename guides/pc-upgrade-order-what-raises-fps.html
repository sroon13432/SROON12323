What to upgrade first on an older or prebuilt PC

Updated 26 November 2026 11 min read 1,464 words · data from 33,392 verified game requirement sets

Most upgrades fail because they start with the graphics card. Work out what is actually limiting the frame rate, spend in this order, and the same budget buys noticeably more.

An older or prebuilt PC usually has one thing holding it back and several things that are merely old. Spending on the wrong one is the most common way to finish an upgrade with the same frame rate and less money, which is why this guide starts with a measurement rather than a shopping list.

The order below is not about which part is most exciting. It is about the cost of each step against the frame time it returns, and about the order that keeps earlier purchases useful when you eventually replace the platform.

First: find what is actually limiting you

Open the game's own overlay or a frame-time tool and watch two numbers while you play for five minutes in a demanding area: GPU utilisation and the busiest CPU thread. If the GPU sits at 95 to 100 percent, you are graphics-bound and the card is the answer. If the GPU drops below about 85 percent while one CPU thread is pinned near 100 percent, the processor is the limit and a faster card will change very little.

Frame time tells the rest of the story. A steady average with regular spikes is usually streaming — storage, memory pressure or shader compilation — rather than raw performance. Those spikes are what most people describe as stutter, and they are the cheapest thing on this list to fix.

The one test worth repeating

Lower the resolution to 720p and keep the settings. If the frame rate barely moves, the CPU is the limit. If it jumps, the GPU is.

Step 1 — Storage (the cheapest real improvement)

If the machine still boots from a hard drive, moving the system and the games to an SSD is the single largest perceived improvement available for the money: load times fall by most of their length, texture streaming stops stuttering, and shader caches stop thrashing. A SATA SSD captures nearly all of it; NVMe is worth the difference only in games built around DirectStorage or when you are already buying.

Check free space at the same time. A drive above about 90 percent full writes more slowly and cannot hold shader caches comfortably, which shows up as stutter in exactly the games that already stream heavily. Our SSD guide covers the SATA-versus-NVMe question in detail.

  • System and most-played game on the SSD first.
  • Keep 10 to 15 percent of the drive free.
  • A second cheap SSD for a game library beats one enormous drive that is always full.

Step 2 — Memory, in the right shape

8 GB is the line where modern games start to stutter regardless of the graphics card: the game, Windows and a browser compete, and the page file joins the frame-time graph. Going to 16 GB in two matched sticks is cheap and fixes more stutter than it fixes average frame rate.

Two sticks matter as much as the capacity. A single 16 GB stick runs in single-channel mode and can cost 10 to 20 percent of frame rate in CPU-heavy games, and far more on a system using integrated graphics. Check the motherboard's slots and the memory generation before buying: DDR4 and DDR5 are not interchangeable, and laptops often use SO-DIMMs.

Step 3 — Graphics card (only if the GPU is the limit)

If the measurement above says the GPU is pinned, the card is where the frame rate comes from. Before buying, check four things: the power supply's wattage and its cables (a modern card may need two or three 8-pin connectors), the case's clearance for length and thickness, whether the motherboard and BIOS will POST with a newer card, and whether the CPU is fast enough to feed it. A very fast card behind a slow processor produces the same frame rate with more headroom and a higher electricity bill.

Match the card to the screen rather than to the box. At 1080p 60 Hz, a mid-range card from the last two generations is enough and the money is better spent elsewhere. At 1440p or 144 Hz, the card becomes the deciding component. The 1080p 60 FPS buying guide lists the cards that meet that target and the ones that overshoot it.

Prebuilt warning

Some prebuilts from large manufacturers use proprietary power supplies and motherboards. Before ordering a card, confirm the PSU has the connectors and the wattage, and that the case takes a standard-length card. A 400 W proprietary unit will not run a modern high-end GPU, and it usually cannot be replaced on its own.

Step 4 — Processor (only when the CPU is the limit)

A CPU upgrade is the step that most often turns into a new computer. Check the socket and the chipset first: the same socket can support several generations, but only with a BIOS update, and only on boards the manufacturer chose to support. If the platform is more than about six years old, the honest comparison is a new CPU, motherboard and memory against a whole new machine.

If the platform is recent enough, a used processor from one or two tiers up is often the best value in PC building: same socket, same cooler, and a large jump in single-core speed, which is what most games actually use. Simulation, strategy and large multiplayer games gain the most; GPU-bound single-player games gain the least.

Step 5 — The free things, which are not nothing

  • Clean the cooler and the fans. A dust-blocked heatsink can cost 10 percent of sustained clock speed and a lot of noise.
  • Set the memory to its rated profile (XMP or EXPO) in the BIOS. Many prebuilts ship at the slow default, and the difference is real in CPU-bound games.
  • Update the graphics driver with a clean install, and check the game's own shader-compilation step is allowed to finish once rather than every launch.
  • Close overlays and background recorders; on an older CPU they cost frame time in exactly the busiest scenes.
  • Check the monitor is actually running at its rated refresh rate in Windows display settings. It is a common and free 60 to 144 Hz upgrade.

When the upgrade is not worth it

Three signals say stop. A DDR3 platform: the memory and the processor are both too old to keep. A four-core, four-thread processor from before 2017: modern games stall on it regardless of the card. A proprietary prebuilt with no standard power connector and no standard motherboard: you can add storage and memory, and that is the end of the road.

In those cases the same budget goes further on a used office machine with a modern six-core processor plus a mid-range card, or on a current entry-level build. Frequently asked questions

Should I upgrade the GPU or the CPU first?

Whichever the measurement says is limiting you. Lower the resolution to 720p: if the frame rate hardly changes, the CPU is the limit and a new GPU will not help; if it rises sharply, the GPU is the limit.

Is 16 GB of RAM enough in 2026?

For gaming, yes, in two sticks. 32 GB helps simulation, strategy and heavy multitasking, and it is the safer choice if you stream or keep many browser tabs open, but it does not raise the frame rate on its own.

Will a new GPU work in an old prebuilt?

Sometimes. Check the power supply wattage and connectors, the case clearance, and whether the BIOS recognises the card. Large-manufacturer prebuilts often use proprietary power supplies that cannot feed a modern GPU.

Does an SSD raise FPS?

It rarely raises the average frame rate, and it removes most of the stutter: shorter loads, faster texture streaming and no more hitches when a game reads a new area. On a machine still running a hard drive, it is the best value upgrade available.

How do I know my memory is running in dual channel?

Two matched sticks in the correct slots, confirmed in a tool such as CPU-Z under the Memory tab, where it should say "Dual" for the channel count. A single stick always reports single channel, whatever its capacity.
