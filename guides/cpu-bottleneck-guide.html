CPU Bottleneck Guide: Is Your Processor Holding Back Your GPU?

Updated 19 November 2026 10 min read 1,213 words · data from 18,489 verified game requirement sets

If your GPU is not pinned at 99 percent, a new graphics card will not help. Here is how to prove where the limit is, and what to upgrade instead.

The most expensive mistake in PC upgrading is buying a graphics card to fix a problem the processor is causing. It happens constantly, because the symptom is identical: the game does not hold the frame rate you expect. The graphics card is the part people know how to replace, so it gets replaced, and the frame rate barely moves.

A CPU bottleneck has a signature and it takes about five minutes to confirm. Once confirmed, the fix is either a settings change, a frame rate cap, or a platform upgrade, and it is worth knowing which before spending anything.

The 60-second test

  1. Open the game at the settings and resolution you actually play at, and get into a demanding scene, not a menu.
  2. Open Task Manager > Performance, or a monitoring overlay, and watch GPU utilisation for a minute.
  3. If GPU utilisation sits at 95 to 99 percent, the graphics card is the limit and a GPU upgrade will help.
  4. If GPU utilisation sits well below 90 percent while the frame rate stays flat, the CPU is the limit and a GPU upgrade will not help.
  5. Confirm with the resolution test: drop to 720p with the same settings. If the frame rate barely rises, the CPU is the wall, because reducing GPU work changed nothing.
Why utilisation is the right metric

A GPU that is not fully used is waiting for something: the CPU to submit work, the memory to deliver data, or the storage to stream assets. The resolution test separates the CPU from the other two in one step.

Reading CPU usage the right way

Overall CPU percentage is nearly useless for this. A game limited by one thread shows 25 percent total usage on a four-core processor, which looks like plenty of headroom and is actually a full core. Look at the per-core graph in Task Manager, or better, at the CPU thread utilisation in HWiNFO or MSI Afterburner: if one or two threads are pinned near 100 percent while the rest idle, that is the signature.

Two further traps. First, Windows spreads work across cores and the display updates slowly, so a brief spike is invisible; set the update speed to High in Task Manager. Second, a CPU can be the limit without any core being at 100 percent, when the limit is memory latency or a driver thread, which is common in games with many draw calls.

The games that are CPU-bound

Some genres are almost always CPU-limited, regardless of the graphics card. Recognising them saves a pointless upgrade:

  • Simulation and city builders. Cities: Skylines II simulates citizens and traffic on the CPU, and the frame rate falls as the city grows even on a top-tier card.
  • Large-scale multiplayer. Rust, Escape from Tarkov and open-world survival games simulate many players, bases and AI agents, and the busiest areas are the slowest.
  • Factory and automation games. Factorio is effectively a CPU benchmark: the limit is updates per second, and graphics settings are irrelevant.
  • Competitive shooters at high frame rates. Counter-Strike 2 at 300 FPS is CPU-bound even on a mid-range card, because the GPU is waiting for draw calls.
  • Racing and truck simulators. Euro Truck Simulator 2 is single-thread limited and has been for a decade; no graphics card fixes a busy city junction.

What actually fixes it

  1. Raise the resolution or the graphics settings. Counter-intuitive but correct: if the CPU is the limit, more GPU work costs nothing and the game looks better for the same frame rate.
  2. Cap the frame rate to something the CPU can hold. A stable 60 is better than 55 to 80 with stutter, and it reduces heat and power.
  3. Close background CPU consumers: browser tabs with video, RGB and monitoring software, and Windows background updates during a session.
  4. Enable Resizable BAR and turn on the correct memory profile (XMP or EXPO) in the BIOS. A CPU running with memory at default speed can lose 10 to 15 percent in CPU-bound games.
  5. Upgrade the CPU within the platform if possible. On AM4, a 5700X3D or 5800X3D transforms CPU-bound performance without a new motherboard. On LGA1700, a 13th or 14th gen part is a drop-in on most boards after a BIOS update.

Why bottleneck calculators mislead

Online bottleneck calculators compare two model names and output a percentage. They cannot know your resolution, the game, the settings, the memory speed or the driver version, and those five variables change the answer more than the part numbers do. The same CPU and GPU pair can be perfectly balanced at 4K and severely CPU-limited at 1080p in an esports title.

The only reliable measurement is your own machine running your own game. Five minutes with a frame time graph beats any calculator, and it gives you the number that actually matters: the frame rate in the scene you care about.

When a platform upgrade is the answer

If the CPU is more than about six generations behind and the socket has no meaningful upgrade path, a new CPU means a new motherboard and new memory, which is most of a new build. In that case the decision is between a whole-platform upgrade and accepting the frame rate you have. Spending on a graphics card first, in that situation, usually produces the disappointing result this guide is about.

If the platform is AM4, AM5 or LGA1700, a CPU-only upgrade is often the best value in PC building: it keeps the memory, the case, the storage and the graphics card, and it fixes exactly the problem described here.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know if my CPU is bottlenecking my GPU?

Watch GPU utilisation while playing. If it stays near 99 percent, the graphics card is the limit. If it sits well below 90 percent while the frame rate is flat, the CPU is the limit. Confirm by dropping the resolution to 720p: if the frame rate hardly changes, the CPU is the wall.

Is a CPU bottleneck dangerous or damaging?

No. It is not a fault, just a description of which component is limiting performance in a given game at a given resolution. It causes no damage and no instability. The only cost is performance you paid for and are not getting.

Will raising graphics settings help a CPU bottleneck?

It will not raise the frame rate, but it will not lower it either, because the GPU has spare capacity. That makes it a free image-quality upgrade: if your CPU caps you at 60 FPS, you may as well run higher settings at the same 60.

Should I trust bottleneck calculator websites?

Not for a purchase decision. They compare model names without knowing your resolution, game, settings or memory speed, and those change the result more than the hardware does. Measure your own machine with a frame time graph instead.
