How to Check If Your PC Can Run Any Game

Updated 16 November 2026 9 min read 1,642 words · data from 9,459 verified game requirement sets

Find your real GPU and RAM, read the requirements page the way a publisher writes it, and know whether you are buying a smooth 60 FPS or a 30 FPS compromise.

Every store page has a requirements block, and almost nobody reads it the way it was written. The minimum column is not a promise of 60 frames per second, the recommended column is not a promise of maximum settings, and the GPU you own is often a different card from the one the publisher named. That gap is where people waste money on an upgrade that changes nothing, or skip a game they could already run comfortably.

Here is the method we use to answer the question for 9,459 games: identify the hardware you have, translate the requirement into a performance tier, then adjust for the resolution and settings you actually use. It takes ten minutes and needs nothing beyond Windows itself.

What a requirements block really promises

Publishers write the minimum column for one target: a playable frame rate at low settings, usually 1080p and usually 30 FPS. The recommended column targets 1080p at 60 FPS on high or medium presets. Neither column states the resolution it was tested at, and when a page says nothing, assume 1080p, because that is what the overwhelming majority of requirement tables are written against.

So a game whose minimum spec you just meet will run, but it will look worse than the screenshots and hover around 30 to 40 FPS. A game you clear at the recommended level is the one that feels like the marketing footage. Decide which of the two you want before comparing a single number.

The one-line rule

Minimum met means it runs. Recommended met means it runs well. Anything in between means low settings, a lower resolution, or an upscaler doing real work.

Step 1 - Write down your exact hardware

You cannot compare hardware you have not identified, and the GPU name has to be exact: a laptop RTX 4060 and a desktop RTX 4060 do not perform alike, and a GTX 1060 with 3 GB is a different card from the 6 GB version despite sharing a name.

For one screen with everything on it, press Win+R, type dxdiag and press Enter: the System tab lists the CPU and memory, the Display tab lists the GPU and its video memory. On Linux, lscpu and lspci -nn | grep VGA give the same picture; on a Mac, About This Mac reports the chip and unified memory.

Step 2 - Compare the GPU by tier, not by name

Requirement tables name a card from the year the game shipped, and that card is often three generations old by the time you read the page. Comparing names fails immediately: an RTX 2070 and an RTX 4060 share no numbers, but the newer 60-class card is comfortably faster. Compare position on a performance ladder instead.

Cards on the same row are roughly interchangeable for this purpose, give or take ten percent. When the requirement names a card one row above yours, expect to drop a preset or lean on an upscaler.

Step 3 - Check VRAM and system RAM separately

These two get confused constantly and they fail differently. VRAM is the memory on the graphics card, and running out does not lower the frame rate politely: textures drop to mush and frame times spike whenever new assets stream in. System RAM is the memory Windows and the game share, and running out of it swaps to disk.

  • Match VRAM to the resolution and texture preset. An 8 GB card at 1080p high is fine in most releases; the same card at 1440p with ray tracing is where 8 GB bites.
  • Match system RAM to the requirement plus a couple of gigabytes. A 16 GB requirement means 16 GB for the game and Windows together.
  • Read the memory figure in Task Manager, not the marketing name. Cards were sold with two memory configurations under one name more than once, and the smaller one is slower in ways the name does not show.

Step 4 - Match the resolutions and settings you will use

Requirements are written against one resolution and one preset, and every step away from that changes the verdict:

Change from 1080p highApproximate cost
1080p high to 1440p high30 to 40 percent fewer frames
1440p high to 4K highroughly double the work of 1440p
High preset to ultra10 to 20 percent, rarely worth it
Ray tracing on at 1080p30 to 60 percent, more in the heaviest titles

A game whose recommended card you exactly meet gives you 1080p high at 60 FPS. Move to a 1440p monitor and the same card is a 40 FPS card until you enable upscaling or drop a preset. Neither the store page nor a bottleneck calculator tells you that, because both ignore your display.

Step 5 - Confirm it will fit and load

Storage is the requirement people skip and then discover the hard way. The listed figure is the installed size before patches, so leave fifteen percent headroom, and note that a growing number of 2026 releases list an SSD as required rather than recommended. A mechanical drive turns asset streaming into visible pop-in even when the frame counter looks healthy.

  • Check the drive type, not just the free space: Windows reports media type per drive under Storage > Advanced storage settings.
  • If space is tight, install on a SATA SSD before a mechanical drive. SATA versus NVMe is a small difference; either versus a hard drive is not.

The five mistakes that cost people money

  1. Upgrading the GPU when the CPU is the limit. If the GPU sits below 90 percent utilisation while the frame rate stays pinned, a new card adds very little.
  2. Buying on the minimum column. Clearing the minimum gets you 30 to 40 FPS at low settings, not the game in the screenshots.
  3. Ignoring the display. A 1440p or 4K panel multiplies the work without changing a number on the store page.
  4. Trusting a bottleneck calculator that knows nothing about your resolution, your settings or the game you play.
  5. Buying 32 GB of RAM before fixing a 4 GB VRAM bottleneck. Only one of those causes hitching.

A 60-second checklist before you buy

  • I know my exact GPU, its dedicated memory, my CPU and my total RAM.
  • I know the resolution I will actually play at.
  • The game's recommended GPU is at or below my card's tier, not just the minimum.
  • My VRAM covers the texture preset I want, and the install drive is an SSD with room to spare.
  • I have checked the game's own page here for its exact published figures.

If every line is true, buy it and expect the recommended experience. If only the minimum is true, expect low settings or a lower resolution. If neither is true, the game will not run at a frame rate you would call playable, and the cheapest fix is usually the display or the settings rather than the hardware.

Frequently asked questions

Can I run a game if I only meet the minimum requirements?

It will launch and be playable, but plan for the publisher's minimum target: usually 1080p at low settings around 30 FPS. Expect to lower the resolution or enable upscaling for a smoother frame rate, and expect it to look noticeably simpler than the screenshots.

Is it worth upgrading a GPU that meets the minimum but not the recommended spec?

Only after checking what limits you. If GPU utilisation sits near 99 percent, the card is the limit and an upgrade helps. If it stays well below that while the frame rate is flat, the CPU or the memory is the limit and a new GPU will change very little.

Does a higher number on the graphics card always mean faster?

No. An RTX 4060 outperforms an RTX 2070 despite the smaller number, and a GTX 1060 3 GB is slower than the 6 GB version of the same card. Compare cards by tier and by the memory they carry, not by the digits in the name.

How accurate are the requirements on Steam?

They are the publisher's own figures and the best official source available, but they are written against one resolution and preset and rarely updated after launch. Treat them as a floor and a target rather than a measurement.
