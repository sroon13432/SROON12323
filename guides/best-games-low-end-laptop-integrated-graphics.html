Best Games for Low-End Laptops and Integrated Graphics

Updated 17 November 2026 10 min read 1,211 words · data from 12,459 verified game requirement sets

A laptop with no discrete GPU is not a dead end. These are the games that run well, the four settings that decide your frame rate, and how to squeeze 60 FPS out of Iris Xe.

Integrated graphics have quietly become good enough for a large part of PC gaming. Intel Iris Xe with 80 or 96 execution units, AMD Radeon 680M and 780M, and the newer Arc integrated parts will all run a substantial library at 60 frames per second, provided you accept the resolution and settings they were designed for. What they will not do is run a 2026 AAA release at 1080p high, and no amount of tweaking changes that.

The useful question is not whether integrated graphics can game, but which games and at what settings. Here is the practical answer, with the settings that actually move the frame counter.

Two hardware facts decide where in that range your laptop lands, and neither appears in the marketing name. First, memory: integrated graphics have no memory of their own, so they read from system RAM, and a single-stick machine gives them half the bandwidth. Second, power limits: the same Iris Xe chip can run at 15 W or 28 W depending on the chassis, and that is worth more than a whole generation of silicon.

Check this first

Open Task Manager > Performance > Memory and look at Slots used. If it says 1 of 2, adding a second stick is the largest single upgrade available to an integrated-graphics laptop, often worth 20 to 40 percent in games.

The four settings that decide your frame rate

Integrated graphics are fill-rate and bandwidth limited, which means a small number of settings dominate everything else. Change these before you touch anything else:

  • Resolution or render scale. Going from 1080p to 900p is a free 25 percent, and to 720p a free 45 percent. Set the game to windowed fullscreen at 900p and let the display scale it, or use the game's own render scale slider if it has one.
  • Shadows. Shadow maps are pure bandwidth. Medium shadows instead of high is routinely worth 15 to 20 percent on integrated hardware.
  • Anti-aliasing. Turn off MSAA and TXAA entirely. If you need smoothing at 720p, use FXAA or the game's temporal upscaler at performance mode.
  • Volumetric fog and screen-space reflections. Both are expensive and both are the first thing to drop. Most games hide them under an advanced or custom preset.

Textures are the opposite case: they cost memory bandwidth but very little compute, and at 720p to 900p most integrated systems have enough memory for medium textures. Keep textures at medium and cut everything from the list above first.

Windows settings worth ten minutes

  • Plug the laptop in. Most thin laptops cut GPU clocks hard on battery, and the difference can be 40 percent.
  • Set the Windows power mode to Best performance, and check the manufacturer utility (Armoury Crate, Lenovo Vantage, MyASUS) for a performance or turbo profile.
  • Cap the frame rate at 60 or at your display's refresh rate. An uncapped frame rate on integrated graphics means constant thermal throttling and worse frame pacing.
  • Lower the in-game resolution rather than the desktop resolution, and use borderless windowed mode so the game does not fight the compositor.
  • Close the browser. On a machine where the GPU shares system memory, four gigabytes of Chrome tabs is four gigabytes the game cannot use.

What to try when a game will not cooperate

  1. Drop to 720p and low. If that is not playable, the game is out of reach on this hardware and no tweak will fix it.
  2. Enable the game's own upscaler at performance mode. FSR 1 and 2 both work on integrated graphics and both recover 30 to 50 percent.
  3. Reduce the internal resolution with a tool like Lossless Scaling if the game has no scaler, and cap at 30 FPS with a consistent frame time rather than chasing 60.
  4. Update the Intel or AMD graphics driver directly from the vendor rather than the laptop maker, which often ships a two-year-old build.
  5. If the game is on a subscription or cloud service, stream it. On a good connection, cloud gaming delivers a better experience than native rendering on a UHD 620 ever will.

A realistic shopping note

If you are buying a laptop and care about games, the specification that matters is not the CPU model but whether there is a discrete GPU and whether the memory runs in dual channel. A Ryzen 7 with a 780M and 16 GB of fast dual-channel memory will outperform a chassis with a low-power discrete card and 8 GB of slow memory, and it will do it on battery. Check the memory configuration on the listing before anything else.

Frequently asked questions

Can Intel Iris Xe run modern games?

It runs esports titles, 2D and indie games, and 3D releases up to roughly 2022 at 720p to 900p low, and it will launch most newer games at low settings with an upscaler. It will not deliver 60 FPS in a 2026 AAA release at 1080p, and no settings change closes that gap.

Is 8 GB of RAM enough for a gaming laptop?

No, unless the games are 2D or esports titles. Integrated graphics allocate memory from the system total, so 8 GB leaves very little for a modern game. 16 GB is the minimum worth buying in 2026, and it also fixes the single-channel problem because it comes as two sticks.

Why does my laptop run games worse on battery?

Manufacturers cut the power limit of the graphics chip on battery to protect the cells, and often drop to a low-power profile on the CPU as well. The difference can reach 40 percent. Plug in for anything that matters, and set the power mode to Best performance.

Is cloud gaming a better option than integrated graphics?

For heavy modern titles, usually yes. A stable connection at 20 Mbps or more gives a better experience than native rendering at 720p low. The trade-off is input latency, which is fine for single-player and adventure games and noticeable in competitive shooters.
